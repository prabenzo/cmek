Key fetch:

Killswitch connecting…

Customer-managed encryption keys (CMEK) let each tenant own the key that protects its data: revoke the key and the provider can no longer read it. This is a multi-tenant webhook queue where every message is sealed under a per-tenant data key that is itself wrapped by that tenant's KMS key, leased for at most 30 s.

The thesis: one tenant's KMS trouble, revocation or traffic surge changes nothing for the other 999, and no plaintext is ever decrypted after a key is revoked. Below, 1,000 tenants across three simulated KMS providers run at about 300 events/s; each card injects a fault or a surge and says what to watch.

This is one shared live system: anyone viewing this page sees the same World, and a scenario started here runs for everyone. Traffic runs only while someone is watching.

Slow KMS

One provider, azure, becomes slow enough that many of its calls run past the timeout, for a minute. What happens to the rest of the service depends entirely on where the key fetch runs, and the three buttons (here and in the header) switch that design live for the whole service, before or during the run. Naive. No lease, no cache, no bulkheads: every event and every delivery makes its own KMS call and waits for it. The slow provider's calls hold the shared delivery workers, so delivery collapses for every tenant, not just azure's, and the KMS call rate explodes. Sync. The cache and the lease are back, so calls are rare again and the bulkhead caps how many are in flight. But the worker that finds a lease due still fetches inline and waits, and every tenant's deliveries queue behind those waits: throughput mostly survives, latency does not. Async. The design that ships. A lease due for renewal is refreshed in the background while deliveries keep using the cached key, so azure's slowness stays azure's problem: its cells flicker yellow, the healthy line stays flat, and the moment you switch to it the backlog drains. Reset world starts over in Async.

Provider outage

One of the three key providers, gcp, stops answering. Every tenant on it depends on that provider to renew its key lease, but each tenant is still holding a cached key, so nothing has to stop right away. Blip. The provider is gone for a few seconds. The affected cells turn yellow while their renewals fail and the service rides through on the leases it already has; when the provider is back, everything is green again and no message was delayed or lost. Outage. The provider is gone for a minute. Yellow spreads first, with no customer impact. Only once a tenant's lease runs out does its cell turn orange: its new events are turned away with a clear error and the messages it already sent wait in the queue, sealed. Retries back off instead of hammering the dead provider. The other two providers' tenants never notice. When gcp returns, the waiting messages deliver.

Key revocation

One busy tenant revokes its key. This is the promise the whole system exists for: from the moment the key is disabled, the provider can no longer read anything of that tenant's, and within a fixed bound it stops trying. Its cell turns purple as soon as the service notices, its new events are refused with an error that says why, the keys it held in memory are purged, and the messages already in the queue stay sealed and waiting. The timeline shows the moment of revocation as the key provider saw it and how long detection took. Nothing else on the grid changes. Click Restore to give the key back: the service notices within seconds and the waiting messages deliver.

Tenant surge

One busy tenant suddenly sends a hundred times its usual traffic for a minute. Its cell stays green and is marked with a ring. Above the rate it is allowed, its extra events are turned away with a rate-limit error, so it cannot crowd out anyone else. Its key traffic does not grow with its event traffic: however many events it sends, the key is renewed on the same slow schedule, which is what the cache and the lease buy. The other tenants' latency does not move.

Global surge

Every tenant sends several times its usual traffic at once, more than the service can deliver. The service does not collapse: delivery holds at its full capacity and the queue absorbs the rest. The overflow is rejected at the door, heaviest senders first, so the tenants sending within their fair share keep getting through untouched. The backlog levels off under its cap and drains once the surge ends. Key traffic stays flat throughout, because a surge of events is not a surge of key fetches.
